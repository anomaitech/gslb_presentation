The GRACE measurement principle

Twin satellites ~220 km apart measure micron-level changes in their separation as gravity, and therefore water mass, varies beneath them. (The 3-D view is unavailable on this display.)

Method 1 · satellite gravimetry

Measuring water mass
from orbit

  • A change in water mass produces a gravity anomaly
  • The anomaly accelerates the lead satellite and the separation changes
  • The microwave link resolves that change at the micron level
  • The effective footprint, however, is ~300 × 300 km, comparable to the basin itself
live ranging signal · Δ separation +0.0 µm nominal separation ≈ 220 km · stretch exaggerated in view

Method 2 · land-surface modeling

Simulating the water balance:
GLDAS-2.2

  • The Catchment Land Surface Model simulates snow, soil moisture, canopy water, and groundwater for each grid cell
  • The 2.2 run assimilates GRACE total-water-storage observations, constraining the simulated storage
  • Provides an explicit groundwater storage variable at 0.25°, daily — far finer than the GRACE footprint
  • Available from February 2003 onward; daily values aggregated to monthly for this analysis
  • Independent of the wells: no in-situ water levels enter the model

Method 3 · workflow detail

From well records
to storage change

  1. Screen the records · ≈1,200 USGS wells; multi-year gaps are pervasive
  2. Impute the gaps · PCHIP for short gaps; an Extreme Learning Machine on climate covariates for long ones
  3. Interpolate · ordinary kriging produces an annual water-level raster for the basin
  4. Difference · ΔGW storage = Sy × Δvolume between consecutive rasters, with Sy = 0.15 · anomalies referenced to the 2004–2009 mean, the GRACE baseline

The pipeline is applied identically in every year, so its assumptions enter all years of the in-situ series alike.

GWDM pipeline: (a) gapped record → (b) imputation → (c) kriged annual rasters → (d) volumetric differencing

Results · climate response

Groundwater lags precipitation by one to two years

The wells put the lag at one to two years (r = 0.59 and 0.60 — a statistical tie) and correlate most strongly with the three-year rainfall average (r = 0.67) — recharge integrates multi-year climate.

Implications for management

One to two years of lead time for drought planning

Precipitation as a leading indicator

Correlations peak at one- to two-year lags: the wells at r = 0.59 and 0.60 (one and two years); the satellite at one year (r = 0.42).

Anticipatory drought response

A dry year signals reduced recharge over the next one to two years, before declines register in wells — pumping and allocation can be adjusted in advance.

A sustained deficit

Inter-drought recovery has been partial: 2011–2016 alone removed ≈10.1 km³, and the anomaly reached −8.7 cm in 2022.

Limitations

Sources of uncertainty

Imputation degrades with gap length

ELM accuracy declines beyond ~3-year gaps; predictions drift toward the climatological mean as gaps lengthen.

0%

Long gaps are prevalent

Roughly half of wells with usable records contain gaps exceeding three years, and a majority of the gap-years fall in the drought years of greatest interest.

Sy · 300km

Structural uncertainty

A single specific yield (0.15) and GRACE's coarse footprint leave absolute volumes uncertain even where the timing agrees.

Future work: an uncertainty analysis — alternative leakage factors, baseline periods, and storage parameters — to bound the reported trends and volumes.

Takeaways
